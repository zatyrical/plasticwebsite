Biofeedback uses measurements such as muscle activity, skin temperature or heart-rate patterns to help a person practise self-regulation. It may be useful for some pain conditions, but evidence for one diagnosis or technique should not be treated as proof of benefit for every kind of chronic pain.

What biofeedback measures

Sensors provide information about a physiological response. EMG biofeedback measures muscle activity; thermal biofeedback measures skin temperature; heart-rate variability biofeedback uses changes in the intervals between heartbeats. These measurements are feedback signals, rather than direct measurements of pain or proof that its underlying cause has been treated. Mayo Clinic’s biofeedback overview explains the different approaches.

What does the research support?

A 2019 evidence map of 16 systematic reviews found consistent evidence of benefit for headache pain, while evidence for many other conditions was insufficient or unclear. Its literature searches ran through September 2018, so it is not a complete assessment of all subsequent research. The relevant question is whether the specific technique has evidence for your diagnosis and the outcome you want to improve.

NCCIH’s relaxation guidance describes research on HRV biofeedback for stress and anxiety. Improvement in stress, a sensor reading or muscle relaxation does not automatically establish sustained pain relief. Avoid assuming that a generic claim about “chronic pain syndrome” applies to every patient.

How it fits into an individual care plan

Biofeedback can be discussed as an additional approach within an assessment-led plan. It should not be presented as universally superior to other care, a cure for the underlying cause of pain, or a reason to stop prescribed treatment. NCCIH advises discussing complementary approaches with your healthcare provider and avoiding their use to postpone medical care.

Questions before starting

Ask the practitioner which diagnosis and technique the proposed programme addresses, what evidence supports it, what qualifications they hold, and how progress will be assessed. Clarify the anticipated cost and review points before committing. Do not assume that a device marketed for wellbeing has been shown to treat your particular pain condition.

Agree on outcomes that matter to you, such as pain interference and day-to-day function, rather than judging success only by a changing sensor display. Discuss the use of home equipment with the practitioner; this page does not endorse a device or establish that biofeedback is offered at our clinic.

Common questions

How quickly will it help? There is no single reliable timeline across diagnoses, techniques and individual circumstances. This page does not promise improvement within a few sessions or weeks.

Will insurance cover it? Coverage depends on your policy, indication and provider. Confirm any authorisation and reimbursement requirements directly with your insurer before treatment.

Does it treat pelvic pain or low back pain? These labels cover different diagnoses. A broad chronic-pain article cannot establish that a particular biofeedback technique is effective for every patient with either symptom; discuss the specific diagnosis and relevant evidence.
