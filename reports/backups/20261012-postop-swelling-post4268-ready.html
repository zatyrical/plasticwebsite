Post-surgery swelling can be expected, but there is no single timeline that distinguishes normal healing from lymphoedema. The pattern must be interpreted against the operation and the recovery plan given by the treating team.

Persistent, progressive, recurrent or asymmetric swelling deserves clinical review because lymphoedema is one possible cause, not the only one. Infection, venous disease and a blood clot may also need consideration. This guide explains what to record, when to contact the operating team and how a lymphoedema assessment may help.

What Is Normal Swelling After Surgery, and How Long Does It Last?

Some swelling is expected after many operations as part of the inflammatory response. The expected amount, distribution and duration depend on the procedure, body region, complications and stage of recovery.

There is no universal day on which postoperative swelling must peak or resolve. Ask the operating team what pattern is expected for your procedure and which postoperative instructions apply to you.

Contact the treating team when swelling is not following that plan, returns after improving, becomes progressively worse, or is accompanied by redness, warmth, fever or increasing pain. Sudden marked swelling, breathlessness or chest pain needs urgent medical assessment.

How Do You Know If Your Swelling Is Lymphedema?

Lymphoedema results from impaired lymphatic drainage and can occur after lymph-node surgery or radiotherapy. It is one cause of postoperative limb swelling; infection, venous problems, a blood clot or tumour-related obstruction may also need consideration. A diagnosis depends on clinical assessment and, when indicated, measurements or imaging, not one symptom alone. The lymphoedema diagnosis guide explains how those findings fit together.

Persistent or progressive swelling, heaviness, tightness or fullness, tighter clothing or jewellery, and skin-texture change can prompt assessment. These features can overlap with other postoperative conditions, so the pattern and medical history matter.

The Stemmer sign may form part of an examination, but it is not a stand-alone diagnosis: false-positive and false-negative results occur, and a negative result does not rule out early disease. See the maintained Stemmer-sign guide for its proper role and limitations.

Important: New or worsening swelling should be discussed with the operating team. Seek urgent medical advice for sudden marked swelling, breathlessness, chest pain, fever, spreading redness or severe pain.

What Are the Early Post-Operative Lymphedema Signs Doctors Look For?

Assessment looks beyond swelling alone. It considers when the change began, how it behaves over time, the operation and lymph-node treatment, skin and tissue changes, symptoms of infection, and other possible causes.

Asymmetry, heaviness, tightness, altered skin texture and recurrent swelling can be clinically relevant, but no single feature confirms lymphoedema. Serial measurements can be more useful than one self-measurement because limb size varies and measurement methods are not fully standardised.

Clinical staging describes the extent of established lymphoedema but does not by itself identify why postoperative swelling is present. The diagnosis and imaging guide explains how history, examination, measurements and imaging may be combined.

Can Lymphoedema Appear After Initial Post-Surgery Swelling Settles?

Lymphoedema can appear during early recovery or later, particularly after lymph-node surgery or radiotherapy. This does not mean ordinary postoperative swelling necessarily “turns into” lymphoedema.

New swelling after an initial recovery period should not automatically be attributed to the old operation. The treating team may need to assess lymphatic, venous, infectious and other causes.

Reporting a changing pattern allows the cause and appropriate next steps to be assessed. Management depends on the diagnosis and may include observation, rehabilitation, compression or specialist investigation; it is not determined from the timeline alone.

What Should You Do If You Are Not Sure Which One You Have?

If the swelling is unexpected or changing, record useful observations and contact the treating team rather than trying to diagnose it from one home test.

  • Record the pattern. Note when swelling began, whether it is improving or worsening, and any heaviness, tightness, pain, redness, warmth or fever.
  • Show change over time. Photographs or symptom notes can help; use the symptom-tracking guide for a structured record.
  • Follow your own postoperative plan. Do not start or alter compression, massage or exercise solely from a web comparison.
  • Escalate an unexpected pattern. Contact the treating team when swelling is progressive, returns after improving or is accompanied by warning symptoms. Seek urgent medical advice for sudden marked swelling, breathlessness, chest pain, fever, spreading redness or severe pain.

These notes can support a clinical assessment, but they do not replace one.
