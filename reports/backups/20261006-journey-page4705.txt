<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">What happens immediately after LVA surgery?</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Lymphaticovenous anastomosis, also called lymphovenous bypass, is a supermicrosurgical procedure that connects selected lymphatic channels to small veins. After surgery, the priority is to protect the wounds and the delicate bypass sites while maintaining safe circulation and limb care. Some patients may have day-surgery or short-stay pathways, while others need longer observation depending on anaesthesia, extent of surgery and medical context.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Compression after LVA surgery</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Compression advice must be individualised. Some patients continue compression garments or bandaging after a defined early period; others may need adjustments based on swelling, wound healing and surgeon preference. LVA should not be framed as a assured way to stop compression permanently.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Activity and return to daily life</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Light walking is often encouraged when safe, but strenuous exercise, heavy lifting, swimming, massage over fresh wounds, or prolonged dependent positioning may need to be avoided until cleared. Return to work depends on the limb treated, job demands, wound healing and symptoms.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">When improvement may be noticed</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Some patients notice changes in heaviness, tightness or infection frequency earlier, while volume changes may take longer and can be subtle. Outcomes vary. Follow-up measurements, symptom tracking and infection history are often more meaningful than a single early impression.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Follow-up and warning signs</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Patients should seek medical advice for fever, spreading redness, increasing pain, wound discharge, sudden swelling, calf pain, breathlessness or other concerning symptoms. Recurrent cellulitis is clinically important and should be discussed during lymphedema follow-up.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->

<!-- wp:heading --><h2 class="wp-block-heading">Frequently asked questions</h2><!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:heading level --><h3 class="wp-block-heading">How long does LVA recovery take?</h3><!-- /wp:post-content -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Early wound recovery may take days to weeks, but lymphedema response is assessed over a longer period. Timelines vary and should be discussed with the treating surgeon.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
<!-- wp:heading level --><h3 class="wp-block-heading">Can I stop compression after LVA?</h3><!-- /wp:heading -->
<!-- wp:paragraph --><p>Not automatically. Compression changes should be guided by clinical response, imaging, measurements and specialist advice.</p><!-- /wp:paragraph -->
