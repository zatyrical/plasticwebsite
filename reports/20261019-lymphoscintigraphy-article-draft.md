## Draft patient article

Lymphoscintigraphy can provide information about lymphatic drainage that is not always available from indocyanine green (ICG) lymphography. Although its images are less detailed for small superficial lymphatic vessels, it can help assess deeper drainage pathways and how tracer reaches lymph nodes. The useful question is not simply which test is better, but what information is needed for your assessment.

### Why was lymphoscintigraphy called the gold standard?

Lymphoscintigraphy has long been used as a reference test for investigating lymphoedema and has often been described as the gold standard. It uses a radioactive tracer and specialised nuclear-medicine imaging equipment to follow lymphatic transport.

That established role does not mean it is the best test for every question. Different imaging methods show different aspects of the lymphatic system. A test that provides a broad view of drainage may not provide the same detail as a test designed to show small vessels near the skin.

### How does it differ from ICG lymphography?

ICG lymphography uses a fluorescent dye and a near-infrared camera to visualise superficial lymphatic drainage. Lymphoscintigraphy uses a radioactive tracer detected by nuclear-medicine equipment. ICG itself does not involve an ionising-radiation scan, although it still requires dye injections and an assessment of suitability.

| Practical question | ICG lymphography | Lymphoscintigraphy |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What is especially useful to see? | Fine detail and patterns in superficial lymphatic drainage. | Broader lymphatic transport, deeper drainage and tracer uptake in lymph nodes. |
| What limits the image? | Fluorescence imaging has limited penetration through tissue. | Lower spatial resolution limits fine detail of small lymphatic vessels. |
| What equipment is needed? | A suitable near-infrared imaging system. | A nuclear-medicine facility and radioactive tracer. |
| Can anatomy be added to the functional information? | It does not provide a CT image. | Selected examinations can include SPECT/CT for anatomical localisation. |

These differences make the tests complementary rather than automatically interchangeable. For more about the fluorescent-dye examination, read the [ICG lymphography guide](https://lymphedasia.com/icg-lymphography-singapore/).

### What are lymphoscintigraphy's limitations?

The main imaging limitation is spatial resolution: it does not show the same fine detail of superficial lymphatic vessels as ICG lymphography. It also requires a radioactive tracer, specialised equipment and access to a nuclear-medicine service.

Comparative studies have found ICG lymphography more sensitive for detecting early lymphoedema in particular patient groups. However, it would be too broad to say that lymphoscintigraphy always has lower sensitivity and specificity. Performance depends on the clinical question, disease stage, protocol and the standard used to judge the result. In one small early upper-limb study, specificity was the same for both tests, while ICG detected more affected limbs.[1,2]

### What useful information can it still provide?

Lymphoscintigraphy can provide functional information about lymph transport and lymph-node uptake, including information from deeper drainage pathways. This is different from obtaining a highly detailed map of small superficial vessels.

Seeing tracer reach a lymph node helps describe drainage to that node; it is not a complete assessment of every aspect of the node's function. The images and report need to be interpreted in the context of the question being investigated.

### What is SPECT/CT?

SPECT stands for single-photon emission computed tomography. It produces three-dimensional nuclear-medicine images of tracer distribution. When combined with CT, the examination is called **SPECT/CT**: the CT component helps locate the tracer findings relative to the body's anatomy.

This can help distinguish the location of deeper and more superficial drainage. Not every lymphoscintigraphy examination includes SPECT/CT; whether it adds useful information depends on the assessment and available imaging protocol.

### When does Dr Jeremy Sun find it useful?

Dr Sun uses lymphoscintigraphy when he needs information that ICG lymphography cannot provide adequately. One situation is assessment of a person with a higher body mass index (BMI), where tissue depth can limit what superficial fluorescence imaging shows.

In that situation, information about deeper drainage and lymph-node uptake may help answer the remaining clinical question. Higher BMI does not automatically make ICG unhelpful, nor does it mean that everyone above a particular BMI needs lymphoscintigraphy. The decision is individualised rather than based on a universal cutoff.

### How should I prepare for a discussion about the scan?

Useful questions include:

- What question would this scan help answer in my assessment?
- What information is still missing after examination or other imaging?
- Would SPECT/CT add useful information in my case?
- How could the result affect the discussion about my care?
- Which instructions should I obtain from the department performing the scan?

Preparation and appointment arrangements should come from the actual imaging department. Another hospital's leaflet should not be treated as your own appointment instructions. If you already have a report, the [diagnosis-report guide](https://lymphedasia.com/lymphedema-diagnosis-report-explained/) provides background reading; individual interpretation requires assessment.

The purpose of imaging is to answer a clinical question and support an appropriate care plan—not to choose a scan simply because it has historically been called the gold standard. A [specialist consultation](https://lymphedasia.com/private-lymphedema-consultation-singapore/) can help clarify which information is relevant to your situation.
